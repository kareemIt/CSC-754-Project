CSC Survey Paper — Master Notes

Smart City Modeling & Simulation Survey

1. Assignment

The project is a survey paper. The goal is to choose a topic, search recent academic literature, catalog the publications, closely read the most relevant papers, classify them, and ultimately write a state-of-the-art survey. The professor recommends using ACM Computing Surveys as the stylistic model.

The final survey should typically contain:

  • 4 foundational papers
  • 8 recent and relevant journal papers
  • 8 recent and relevant conference papers / technical reports

2. Our Topic

Modeling and Simulation for Smart Cities: Methods, Applications, and Emerging Technologies

Central research question

How are modeling and simulation techniques used to understand, design, optimize, and manage smart cities?

Rather than making the survey exclusively about digital twins, we're treating digital twins as one important component of the broader smart-city modeling and simulation field.

3. Half-Page Topic Description

Modeling and Simulation for Smart Cities: Methods, Applications, and Emerging Technologies

As cities continue to grow and face more and more complex infrastructural, energy, transportation, and environmental challenges. So, with the use of smart city simulation, we will be able to understand, design, optimize, and help manage these cities better without the need to test these solutions in the real world.

In this survey, we will be looking at major modeling/simulation methods used in smart city research. This may include agent-based modeling, discrete-event, system dynamics, and data-driven simulation. It will also look into transportation and traffic management alongside energy and smart grids, as well as new and emerging technologies such as digital twins and AI-driven solutions.

The goal of this survey is primarily to classify and compare different approaches to the solutions that current cities are facing and weigh the pros and cons of each approach as we try to determine which set of approaches would be most effective at solving these issues.

4. Planned Classification Scheme

This is currently our working classification for organizing the literature.

  1. I. Foundations & Urban Modeling Architectures
    General smart-city modeling frameworks, urban digital twins, integrated modeling platforms, definitions and architectures.
  2. II. Modeling & Simulation Methods
    Agent-based modeling, system dynamics, numerical/physics-based simulation, discrete-event simulation, microsimulation, cellular models, data-driven simulation.
  3. III. Transportation & Urban Mobility
    Traffic flow, autonomous vehicles, multimodal transportation, routing, congestion, evacuation, and mobility digital twins.
  4. IV. Energy & Buildings
    Urban energy consumption, smart grids, building energy simulation, microclimates, regional energy systems, and energy digital twins.
  5. V. Environment & Urban Resilience
    Flooding, stormwater, climate change, waste management, disaster response, and infrastructure resilience.
  6. VI. AI & Data-Driven Simulation
    Machine learning, generative AI, IoT/sensor integration, real-time models, and intelligent digital twins.
  7. VII. Challenges & Future Research
    Interoperability, model accuracy, scalability, data quality, privacy, standardization, real-time integration, validation, and future research.

6. Selected 25 Main Papers for Thorough Reading

1. Foundational Papers (4)

  • Smart City Based on Digital Twins (Li, Deren et al., 2021)

    • Summary

    This paper goes in depth about how digital twins can serve as the foundation for smart city development by creating a two-way connection between the physical city and the virtual city. It primarily focuses on smart city planning, management, and governance rather than on simulation specifically. The resources discussed in this paper are IoT, sensors, big data, cloud computing, AI, 5G, GIS, and simulation. The researcher describes five major applications: the smart city operation brain, traffic management, public safety, public services, and epidemic management. A limitation of this paper is that it is based on conceptual frameworks and a review of applications, so there is no data present. It is argued that future smart cities need stronger spatial information infrastructure, better integration of urban data, and comprehensive city operation centers to fully realize digital twin-based management.

  • Digital Twins in City Planning (Michael Batty, 2024)

    • Summary

    This paper is a perspective review on how digital twins can support city planning rather than a single implementation of a digital twin study case. It looks at urban modeling approaches, including aggregate economic and behavioral models, agent-based models, cellular models, and microsimulations, and discusses how they can be connected to digital twins efficiently. The application is urban planning and smart city decision support, with digital twins helping to plan, understand, predict, and design changes to cities. The scale of this paper ranges from local neighborhoods to entire systems of cities, with an emphasis on keeping people, policymakers, and scientists in the loop rather than allowing the digital twin to make decisions independently. Since this is a perspective paper, it does not present any real data set. The main contribution is to explain how existing urban models can form the foundations of city digital twins while highlighting challenges such as data integration, model complexity, computational requirements, and representing complex human behavior. The researcher argues that future work should focus on developing more integrated and scalable urban digital twins that combine different models and data sources.

  • A Systematic Review of a Digital Twin City: A New Pattern of Urban Governance Toward Smart Cities (2021)

    • Summary

    This paper goes in depth on how digital twin cities can transform urban governance and support smart city development rather than building a specific simulation. The author covers multiple concepts, technologies, and potential applications of digital twin cities, including IoT, 5G, blockchain, collaborative computing, simulations, and artificial intelligence. The goal is to improve how cities monitor conditions, analyze information, simulate scenarios, and make decisions. Since this is a review rather than an implementation model, there is no specific dataset or traditional simulation validation. Limitations of this paper are that it is all conceptual rather than an operational case study. The outcome from the paper was that future work should focus on better data integration, real-time interaction, interoperability, security, AI, and developing more comprehensive digital twin city systems.

  • Digital Twin-Supported Smart City: Status, Challenges and Future Research Directions (Wang et al., 2023)

    • Summary

    This research paper is a systematic literature review of how digital twins are being used to support smart cities rather than a single simulation or case study. The author states that he reviewed 202 papers and examined how digital twins use technologies such as IoT, sensors, machine learning, cloud computing, simulation, data management, and 3D visualization across different smart city applications. The main purpose is to gain a better understanding of the current state of the field, identify challenges, and guide future research and decision-making. The argument the researcher provides is that future smart city digital twins need better real-time data integration, interoperability, modeling capabilities, security, and decision-support mechanisms to move from separate technologies toward a more integrated city management system.

The simulation was conducted in Las Palmas de Gran Canaria, Spain, using census and demographic data, as well as tourism activity. Its purpose is to test situations and scenarios relevant to decision-making. The simulation is fast: it can simulate 19 hours in 20 minutes. The study reports 96% accuracy in recognizing user intentions and mapping them to simulation configurations. A major limitation is that this is still a TRL-4 proof of concept and is not yet ready for deployment as a large-scale city digital twin. Future work could focus on real-world deployment and improving the available urban data.

3. Recent Conference Papers & Technical Reports (8)

  • Agent-Based Modeling of a Stadium Evacuation in a Smart City (Zhang et al., 2018)

    • Summary

    This paper focuses on improving emergency evacuation in a stadium by studying how communication, misinformation, and first responders' actions affect people's behavior. It uses an agent-based modeling (ABM) approach in NetLogo, where people in the stadium are modeled as agents. The model incorporates decision-making, human behavior, the stadium layout, and historical stadium data. The main takeaway is that miscommunication and slower first responders can lead to less predictable outcomes. One strength of the ABM approach is its ability to visualize how crowd congestion forms. A limitation is that the study focuses on a single stadium rather than an entire city, though its findings still offer useful insights. Future work could explore ways to improve first-responder response and communication and expand the simulation to a larger scale.

7. Main Argument Beginning to Emerge

We haven't finalized a thesis yet, but the literature is pointing toward a useful story:

Smart-city simulation is evolving from relatively isolated and static urban models toward integrated, data-driven, continuously updated systems.

The progression we may eventually discuss is approximately:

Traditional simulation → integrated urban modeling → sensor/IoT-connected models → digital twins → AI-enhanced and increasingly real-time simulation.

At the same time, older simulation methods aren't simply disappearing. Agent-based modeling, system dynamics, numerical simulation, microsimulation, etc. increasingly operate inside larger digital-twin/smart-city platforms.

This distinction could become one of the central ideas of our final survey.

8. Questions We'll Compare Across Papers

  • Research problem — What problem is being solved?
  • Application/domain — Transportation, energy, flooding, planning, etc.
  • Modeling technique — Agent-based, system dynamics, numerical, ML/data-driven, etc.
  • Simulation purpose — Prediction, optimization, scenario testing, decision support, monitoring, etc.
  • Data — Historical, synthetic, IoT sensors, GIS, cameras, building data, etc.
  • Scale — Building, neighborhood, transportation network, or entire city.
  • Real-time capability — Static/offline vs continuously updated.
  • Validation — How do the authors establish that their model works?
  • Main results — What did they actually discover?
  • Strengths — Why is the approach useful?
  • Limitations — What doesn't it solve?
  • Future work — What do the authors say needs to happen next?

These will become our literature matrix.
